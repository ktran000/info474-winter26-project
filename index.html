The Geography of Happiness
INFO 474 Project

What makes a country happy? The answer isn't as simple as wealth. Finland has topped the global happiness rankings for seven consecutive years — yet it's not the richest country in the world. Meanwhile, some of the wealthiest nations are falling behind. This interactive story digs into the data behind global wellbeing, exploring how economic strength, social support, life expectancy, and personal freedom each play a role. What you find might challenge everything you assumed about happiness.

Scroll to explore how these variables relate to happiness.
A World Divided
Take a look at the map. The pattern is hard to miss: happiness clusters geographically. Northern Europe glows brightest, with Finland, Denmark, and their neighbors consistently topping the rankings. Sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia sit at the other end of the spectrum. But is this simply a story about wealth? The northern countries are prosperous, yes — but so are parts of East Asia and the Gulf States, which don't rank nearly as high. Before jumping to conclusions, let's look more closely at the relationship between a country's income and how happy its people actually report feeling.
GDP and Happiness
Wealthier countries do tend to report higher happiness. But look closer at the spread. Western Europe (blue) clusters confidently at the top right, while Sub-Saharan Africa (gray) sits concentrated at the bottom left. Yet the middle of the chart tells a more interesting story: Latin American countries (purple) rank surprisingly high for their income level, while several Middle Eastern and East Asian nations with comparable GDPs score notably lower. Wealth opens the door to happiness, but it doesn't guarantee it. So what separates the overachievers from the underachievers?

The gap between the top and bottom regions is nearly 3 full points, a difference that no single factor can explain on its own.
Freedom and Well-Being
Hover over points to see individual countries.

GDP explains a lot, but it doesn't explain everything. Freedom to make life choices is one of the most consistent predictors of happiness across regions, regardless of income level. The trend seems linear: countries where people feel they have genuine autonomy over their lives tend to report significantly higher wellbeing. And unlike wealth, freedom doesn't plateau; its relationship with happiness holds strong across the entire range. This helps explain some of the outliers from earlier: Latin American countries, despite modest GDPs, score high on personal freedom, while some wealthier authoritarian states cluster well below the trend line. Money buys comfort but agency seems to shapes how people actually feel about their lives.
Health and Happiness
The world's happiest countries share more than good policies, their people live longer too. Hover over any bar and you'll find life expectancy tucked alongside the happiness score, and the pattern is striking: nearly every country in this top 15 reports a healthy life expectancy above 70 years. The Nordic nations lead the pack, combining high scores with strong public health systems built on universal access and social investment. But the most intriguing entry is at the bottom of the list: Costa Rica is the only Latin American country to break into the global top 15, achieving happiness scores that rival far wealthier nations. It's a reminder that longevity and wellbeing aren't just products of wealth; they're products of how a society chooses to take care of its people.

What We Learned
This data offers a window, not a complete answer. Across the regions, a few patterns emerge consistently: higher GDP tends to correlate with higher happiness, but the relationship weakens at the top. Freedom to make life choices and social support appear alongside high happiness scores more reliably than wealth alone. And outliers like Costa Rica remind us that the full picture is always more complex than any single variable can capture. These are correlations, not causes. But they point to something worth sitting with: the factors most associated with wellbeing aren't just economic. How societies support their people (through trust, health systems, and personal freedom) seems to matter just as much as how wealthy those societies are.
